Cardiorespiratory Fitness

VO₂ Max & Why It Matters

VO₂ max is the main measure of cardiorespiratory fitness. Simply put, your VO₂ max score indicates how good your body is at utilizing oxygen during exercise.

So why is VO₂ max so important? Higher VO₂ max scores are associated with overall health improvements and reduced all-cause mortality. Studies have found VO₂ max to be more predictive of longevity than factors like smoking, diabetes, and coronary artery disease.

Measuring your VO₂ max is not necessary. The main takeaway is the positive effects that improved cardiovascular fitness can have on your lifespan. If interested, estimating VO₂ max can be done with simple tests or a smartwatch/fitness tracker.

Essential Principles

  • Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of lifespan—prioritize it.
  • Find movement you enjoy; consistency beats the perfect protocol.
  • Most sessions at easy–moderate intensity (conversation pace); add some higher-intensity work weekly.
  • Ignore fad zone jargon unless you have a specific performance goal.

Improving Cardiorespiratory Fitness

Please Note: If you have any cardiac issues, consult a healthcare professional before engaging in rigorous physical activity.
  • Find what you enjoy: Dancing, hiking, tennis, spin—anything that elevates heart rate for a sustained period works.
  • Make it entertaining: Podcasts, music, TV on the treadmill, or a training partner for accountability and enjoyment.
  • Don't get bogged down in jargon: Terms like "fat burning zone" or "zone 3 cardio" have limited practical use for most people.
  • The ideal intensity mix: Spend most time at easy–moderate intensity; include some higher-intensity sessions to challenge your cardiorespiratory system.
02

Strength Training & Muscle Mass

Studies show a significant correlation between muscle mass, strength, and mortality. Whether direct cause or knock-on effect, the outcome is clear: improved muscle mass and strength help you stay physically active as you age, reducing mortality risk and improving quality of life.

Strength training prevents osteoporosis, and increased muscle mass aids weight management by raising your BMR—the calories your body burns at rest each day.

Essential Principles

  • Train each muscle group twice per week (two full-body sessions is enough).
  • Train within 3 reps of failure on every set—intensity drives adaptation.
  • Apply progressive overload each block: more reps, sets, or weight.
  • Use solid technique; 8–12 reps × 2–3 sets is a reliable default.
  • Rest at least one minute between sets; at least one full day before training the same muscles again.

Who Should Be Strength Training?

Everyone.

  • Age-related decline: Muscle loss can begin as early as thirty—approximately 3–8% per decade after 30.
  • The risk of inaction: Unaddressed loss leads to difficulty with daily tasks, fragility, falls, and loss of independence.
  • It's never too late: You can slow decline, build a base now, or improve strength and function at any age.
  • Cardiovascular support: Muscle preserves your ability to train your heart and lungs—the biggest lifespan indicators.

How to Strength Train

Weight training is simple when you focus on five core principles: Frequency, Intensity, Progressive Overload, Technique, and Rest.

1. Frequency

  • Train each muscle group twice a week—two 45–60 minute full-body sessions with compound movements is plenty.

2. Intensity

  • Push close to failure—the point where you could not do another rep with good form.
  • Occasionally take a set to absolute failure to calibrate effort; otherwise stay within 3 reps of failure.

3. Progressive Overload

  • Add reps, sets, or weight over time so intensity stays high as you adapt.

4. Technique

  • Lift explosively, lower under control (at least one second on the eccentric).
  • Rep count matters less than proximity to failure; 8–12 reps × 2–3 sets is a practical default.

5. Rest

  • Rest at least one minute between sets; one full day before retraining the same muscles.
  • Prioritize sleep and protein in recovery. Soreness is not required for growth.

How to Make Strength Training Enjoyable

  • Music, progress tracking (e.g. RepCount), gym-only podcasts or shows.
  • A gym buddy for fun and accountability; reward yourself after sessions (e.g. coffee shop ritual).

Fat Loss Dynamics

Fat loss is not a moral project—it is physics plus patience. You need a sustained calorie deficit: you consume slightly fewer calories than you burn over time. The mistake most people make is chasing speed; aggressive deficits destroy adherence, muscle, and energy.

Sustainable fat loss looks like a gentle downward trend on the scale and in how clothes fit—not a dramatic drop in week one followed by a rebound. Track trends over weeks, not single days. Daily weight fluctuates with water, salt, sleep, and stress.

Essential Principles

  • Create a gentle calorie deficit—small enough to sustain for months.
  • Aim for slow trends (roughly 0.25–0.75% body weight per week for many people).
  • Keep protein high and keep lifting—muscle is the asset you are protecting.
  • Use data (scale trend, photos, measurements)—not guilt or miracle weeks.

Building Your Deficit

  • Start with food: Reduce portions slightly, swap calorie-dense extras, or structure meals—don't slash everything at once.
  • Add movement if needed: Extra steps or cardio can help, but diet does most of the work; don't out-train a poor deficit.
  • Diet breaks & maintenance: Planned higher-calorie weeks can improve adherence and hormones on long cuts—consultancy clients get this mapped to their calendar.

What to Ignore

  • Detox teas, crash diets, and "metabolism hacks" without evidence.
  • Daily weigh-ins as pass/fail—look at rolling averages.
  • Cutting carbs or fats entirely—unnecessary for most people (see Nutrition).
04

Nutrition

Simple Rules That Scale

Nutrition culture thrives on fear and extremes. The basics are boring—and that is why they work. You do not need a branded diet; you need a handful of habits you can repeat for years.

Essential Principles

  • Prioritize protein at every meal—anchor for muscle, satiety, and recovery.
  • Keep it simple—whole foods most of the time; flexibility the rest.
  • Carbs are not the enemy—they fuel training and daily life; match intake to activity.
  • Enjoy your food—adherence beats perfection; social meals are part of a real life.

Protein First

  • Target a consistent daily protein intake aligned with your goals (often ~1.6–2.2 g per kg body weight for active people—adjust with a professional if needed).
  • Distribute across meals; lean meats, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, and quality powders all count.

Carbs, Fats & Flexibility

  • Carbohydrates support hard training and cognitive work—reduce them when in a deficit if calories require it, not because they are "bad."
  • Dietary fats are essential for hormones and health—include nuts, olive oil, avocados, and fatty fish.
  • Ignore fad eliminations unless you have a medical reason; consistency and total calories drive fat loss.

Enjoyment & Adherence

  • Plan meals you actually want to eat; rigid meal plans fail when life gets busy.
  • Restaurant and social eating are normal—bank slightly lighter meals elsewhere or order protein-forward options.
  • Food is fuel and pleasure; neither extreme serves long-term health.
