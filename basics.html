Strength Training & Muscle Mass

The Importance of Muscle Mass

Studies show a powerful connection between muscle mass, strength, and a longer life. While scientists debate the exact cause, the practical reality is undeniable: more muscle equals a better quality of life.

As we age, strength training becomes our ultimate insurance policy to a longer, happier and healthier life.

Why You Need to Strength Train

  • Maintain Independence: Age-related muscle loss begins as early as 30, decreasing by 3–8% per decade and speeding up as we get older. Strength training slows this process, preventing frailty, falls, and a loss of independence.
  • Boost Metabolism: Muscle is metabolically active. More muscle mass increases your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), meaning your body burns more calories every day, even at rest.
  • Protect Your Bones: Lifting weights is one of the most effective ways to prevent osteoporosis (loss of bone density) which is especially prevalent in women.
  • Protect Your Cardiorespiratory Fitness: Preserving muscle ensures you stay mobile enough to train your cardiorespiratory system—the single biggest indicator of lifespan.

Bottom Line

It is never too late to start. No matter your age, you can improve your strength, protect your body, and reduce physical decline.

The Core Philosophy: Chase Stimulus, Not Fatigue

When you exercise, your primary goal shouldn't just be to sweat, burn calories, or get tired. The ultimate goal of training is to stimulate adaptation.

Your body is incredibly smart, but it is also lazy—it will not build energy-costly muscle unless you give it an undeniable reason to. Training is simply the act of sending a clear message to your body that its current level of strength isn't enough, forcing it to adapt and grow stronger.

To get the best results, you must focus on chasing stimulus, not fatigue:

  • Chasing Stimulus (The Goal): Lift close to failure (the point where you couldn't possibly do another rep) to stimulate your body to adapt. Progressive overload from session to session in order to continue stimulating adaptation. Try to add 1 rep or a tiny bit of weight each session. (Note: you won't be able to do this every session, but it is always the goal.)
  • Chasing Fatigue (The Trap): This is the common mistake of thinking a workout is only successful if you leave the gym completely exhausted, destroyed, or unable to walk. Fatigue is just a byproduct of exercise—it is not proof of a good workout.

How to Strength Train

Fitness advice online can be incredibly confusing, filled with complicated training splits and "must-do" exercises. In reality, effective training is very simple if you just focus on a few key things.

  1. Frequency

    Aim to train each muscle group twice a week, This can easily be accomplished by two full body days. Sessions do not have to be long. Two 45-60 minute workouts focussing on compound movements (movements involving multiple muscles) is plenty.

  2. Intensity

    Intensity is the thing that people get wrong most often. Workouts need to be challenging. You must push close to the point of failure (failure = point where you could not possibly do another repetition).

    Often people overestimate how hard they are working. e.g. they stop at 10 reps thinking that's all they can do when really they could do closer to 15. For this reason, once confident with the exercise technique, I believe you should take a set of each exercise (that you can perform safety) to failure at least once every few sessions so that you know where your point of failure is.

    You should then train to or close to (within 3 reps of) this point of failure every set. Your body does not want to build muscle it is a costly process and it requires energy (calories) to maintain muscle mass. When we strength train we are essentially showing our bodies that we are incapable of completing this task (lifting the weight) and therefore need to adapt, build more muscle and get stronger. If we do not train hard, we do not stimulate this adaptation.

  3. Progressive Overload

    Progressive overload links closely with the above point on intensity. Progressive overload essentially means trying to train a little bit harder each session. This can be accomplished by trying to add reps, sets or weight. Progressive overload is key to ensuring intensity remains high, if training is effective what was hard at the start of the training block is likely not hard now.

  4. Technique

    Ensuring proper technique will help you avoid injury and ensure you are training the targeted muscles. Ask a personal trainer to check your technique or watch a video on YouTube to ensure you are performing the exercise properly.

    When it comes to tempo (speed of lifts), focus on contracting fast and controlling the lengthening phase of the movement, taking at least a full second. In a squat this would look like controlling the way down and standing back up fast.

    Note: A slower eccentric (lowering/ lengthening) phase does not increase muscle mass to a greater degree than a shorter controlled eccentric phase.

    Rep ranges and sets: The number of repetitions you do of each exercise doesn't matter that much as long as you are pushing each set close to failure. The general recommendation is between 8-12 reps for 2-3 sets. How many sets you do per muscle group will vary greatly depending on your training split.

  5. Rest

    Rest and recovery is key, in between sets take at least one minute rest. Listen to your body and rest as long as it needs, the more time between sets the better.

    Take at least one days rest between training each muscle group. During this time prioristise sleep and eating a protein rich diet. Note: when you first start training, your muscles will be very sore after working out this is normal. Over time the feelings of fatigue will reduce.

Remember

Muscle damage/ muscle pain does not drive muscle growth. You do not need to be sore after a workout for it to be effective.

How to Make Strength Training Enjoyable

Enjoyment is the key to consistency. Strength training can feel pretty repetitive compared to other forms of activity, but there are plenty of ways to make it more fun:

  • Listen to music
  • Track your progress — it boosts motivation, you don't always realise the progress you're making and helps you progressive overload. Try an app like RepCount.
  • Have a "gym only" podcast or showthat you watch/listen to while training. This way you look forward to going to the gym to watch that next episode.
  • Find a gym buddy — it's more fun and you'll keep each other accountable.

Create a positive routine around training — e.g. Maybe you don't like going to the gym but you do love reading in the coffee shop nearby. By combining these (Going to the cafe after you workout) you can create a positive training experience, and again you'll look forward to going to the gym.

Cardiorespiratory Fitness

VO₂ Max & Why It Matters

VO₂ max is the ultimate measure of your cardiorespiratory fitness. Simply put, your VO₂ max score indicates the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in, transport, and utilize during exercise.

A good analogy for VO₂ max is your body’s engine size. It isn't just an elite fitness metric for athletes; it is the combined output of your lungs moving air, your heart pumping blood, and your muscles converting that oxygen into energy. If any of these systems begin to decline, your VO₂ max drops.

Because it measures your entire body’s systemic health, it is a staggering indicator of life expectancy:

  • The Ultimate Predictor: Large, long-term clinical studies show that your cardiorespiratory fitness is more predictive of how long you will live than traditional risk factors like smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and type 2 diabetes combined.
  • The Longevity Gap: Moving from the lowest fitness category to an above-average category has been shown to cut your risk of early death by roughly 50%.

Do I need to test it?

Absolutely not. While you can estimate it using a smartwatch or fitness tracker, you don't need a lab test to reap the benefits. The target isn't a specific number; the target is simply moving your current baseline upward.

How to Train Your Cardiorespiratory System

If you have any underlying medical or cardiac issues, consult a healthcare professional before beginning a rigorous exercise routine.

Otherwise, improving your heart and lung health comes down to consistency over perfection.

Find Cardio You Enjoy

Any form of activity can be used to improve your cardiorespiratory system. The key is to find something you enjoy so it doesn't feel like a chore. Dancing, hiking, playing tennis, cycling, swimming, or brisk walking are all great. Anything that elevates your heart rate for a sustained period of time works.

If you can’t find a sport or activity you love, build an entertaining environment around it. Watch your favorite TV show on a treadmill, save a specific highly engaging podcast exclusively for your walks, or recruit a friend so you have built-in accountability and social time.

Ditch the Zones: The 80/20 Rule

Fitness influencers love to overcomplicate cardio with phrases like the "fat-burning zone" or "Zone 3 cardio." For the average person, these terms create unnecessary confusion. Your body burns fat and improves fitness across all intensities.

Instead of tracking complicated heart rate zones, simplify your training by structuring your week into an 80/20 intensity mix:

  • The 80% (Easy & Moderate Intensity): Spend the vast majority of your cardio time here. Your heart rate should be elevated, but you should easily be able to maintain a conversation without gasping for breath (often called the "talk test"). This form of cardio provides immense health benefits without draining your daily energy. This "easy" cardio can be anything you like; running, walking, dancing, hiking, playing tennis, spin classes. If it elevates your heart rate, it works.
  • The 20% (High-Intensity Intervals): Once or twice a week, include a brief session where you push hard enough to challenge your lungs, making a conversation completely impossible. This is the stimulus that forces your maximum engine size (VO₂ max) to expand.

How to Use High-Intensity Intervals

The most effective way to tackle that 20% high-intensity work is through interval training (alternating periods of hard work with periods of rest). Depending on your goals, you can tweak the length of your intervals to chase different adaptations:

  • Short Intervals (Under 2 Minutes): Pushing at maximum effort for short bursts primarily targets and improves your anaerobic system (your power and sprint capacity).
  • Longer Intervals (2 to 5 Minutes): Sustaining a hard, breathless effort for slightly longer blocks provides the absolute greatest stimulus for expanding your VO₂ max and overall cardiovascular endurance.

Keeping it Simple

If you want to take your training to an higher level later on, you can design specific protocols for specific adaptations. But for the average person looking to live a long, vibrant life? Master the 80/20 mix, find a type of movement you actually enjoy, and you have already won.

The Strength Connection

Preserving your muscle mass through strength training is what keeps your joints and framework healthy enough to perform cardio as you age. Together, they form the ultimate insurance policy for your health span.

Nutrition

Simple Rules That Scale

Nutrition culture thrives on fear and extremes. The basics are boring—and that is why they work. You do not need a branded diet; you need a handful of habits you can repeat for years.

Essential Principles

  • Prioritize protein at every meal—anchor for muscle, satiety, and recovery.
  • Keep it simple—whole foods most of the time; flexibility the rest.
  • Carbs are not the enemy—they fuel training and daily life; match intake to activity.
  • Enjoy your food—adherence beats perfection; social meals are part of a real life.

Protein First

  • Target a consistent daily protein intake aligned with your goals (often ~1.6–2.2 g per kg body weight for active people—adjust with a professional if needed).
  • Distribute across meals; lean meats, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, and quality powders all count.

Carbs, Fats & Flexibility

  • Carbohydrates support hard training and cognitive work—reduce them when in a deficit if calories require it, not because they are "bad."
  • Dietary fats are essential for hormones and health—include nuts, olive oil, avocados, and fatty fish.
  • Ignore fad eliminations unless you have a medical reason; consistency and total calories drive fat loss.

Enjoyment & Adherence

  • Plan meals you actually want to eat; rigid meal plans fail when life gets busy.
  • Restaurant and social eating are normal—bank slightly lighter meals elsewhere or order protein-forward options.
  • Food is fuel and pleasure; neither extreme serves long-term health.

Fat Loss Dynamics

Fat loss is not a moral project—it is physics plus patience. You need a sustained calorie deficit: you consume slightly fewer calories than you burn over time. The mistake most people make is chasing speed; aggressive deficits destroy adherence, muscle, and energy.

Sustainable fat loss looks like a gentle downward trend on the scale and in how clothes fit—not a dramatic drop in week one followed by a rebound. Track trends over weeks, not single days. Daily weight fluctuates with water, salt, sleep, and stress.

Essential Principles

  • Create a gentle calorie deficit—small enough to sustain for months.
  • Aim for slow trends (roughly 0.25–0.75% body weight per week for many people).
  • Keep protein high and keep lifting—muscle is the asset you are protecting.
  • Use data (scale trend, photos, measurements)—not guilt or miracle weeks.

Building Your Deficit

  • Start with food: Reduce portions slightly, swap calorie-dense extras, or structure meals—don't slash everything at once.
  • Add movement if needed: Extra steps or cardio can help, but diet does most of the work; don't out-train a poor deficit.
  • Diet breaks & maintenance: Planned higher-calorie weeks can improve adherence and hormones on long cuts—consultancy clients get this mapped to their calendar.

What to Ignore

  • Detox teas, crash diets, and "metabolism hacks" without evidence.
  • Daily weigh-ins as pass/fail—look at rolling averages.
  • Cutting carbs or fats entirely—unnecessary for most people (see Nutrition).
